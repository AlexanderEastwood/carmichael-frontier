"We were deep at the wrong λ; you found the right one" was loose shorthand from my side of a conversation with the assistant, not a description of your algorithm, and it misrepresents it: your search exhausts preproducts P and derives λ(P) from them; it does not choose λ first. The paper itself never characterised your method; the one sentence about your number says it "supplied a second modulus to investigate", which is exactly what happened.

On the substance, I agree with you, and I want to state precisely what our computation does and does not establish:

  • Two restricted minima. N₀ is the least 64-factor Carmichael number with λ(n) | λ(N₀), and your N is the least with λ(n) | λ(N). Each rests on a complete search of the corresponding divisor family (every D | M that can carry such a number; its own 64-smallest base; every size-feasible exchange radius; the residue condition n ≡ 1 mod D), with per-modulus records and an independent coverage checker in the repository. Consequently any 64-factor Carmichael number below N has λ(n) ∤ λ(N₀) and λ(n) ∤ λ(N) — and that is all they say.
  • No global claim, and no λ-first algorithm. A complete λ-first enumeration exists in principle — every competitor has λ(n) dividing L = lcm{q−1 : q ≤ (N−1)/C₆₃} — but it is hopeless: L has on the order of 1010 030 divisors, more than 1010 000 of them pass the pool-size and cheapest-product filters, and even the crude bound λ(n) < n/2⁶³ leaves at least ~10⁶⁵ candidates. So we have no efficient modulus cover, no evidence that a family search competes with preproduct exhaustion for global certification, and we make no such claim. A restricted-family exclusion also does not by itself eliminate a preproduct subtree in your search; that would need a separate coverage argument.
  • What we think it is good for, next to yours. Exhaustive certification inside a specified family (minutes per modulus); heuristic searches for tighter incumbents — we swept one-step neighbours of both λ's (about 50,000 moduli at small exchange radii) and found nothing below N, a bounded negative, nothing more; and independent positive controls (with the bound frozen at the previous incumbent, the pipeline rediscovers N₀ at radius 8 and your N at radius 6 from the 64 smallest eligible primes — a test of our implementation, not of completeness).

I've added a sentence to §2.1 saying exactly this — that the divisor-family computations do not cover all Carmichael functions below N and therefore certify no global minimality, and that your exhaustive preproduct search is designed to provide that coverage (citing Shallue–Webster, Algorithms for Carmichael numbers, §5 and §7.4) — with the status of your k = 64 run attributed to your communication.
