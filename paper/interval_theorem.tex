\begin{abstract}
Let $S_k$ denote the smallest Carmichael number with exactly $k$ distinct prime factors (OEIS
\href{https://oeis.org/A006931}{A006931}; known for $k\le 63$, open beyond). We give certified lower
and upper bounds on $S_k$ for every $k$ from $64$ to $144$, each with a reproducible certificate and
exact endpoints, and quantify the improvement over the identified previous bound. No claim of priority
is made; the bounds stand on the computations. The sharpest cases are $k=64$ and $k=65$. For $k=64$ the
upper bound is a $148$--digit Carmichael number $N$ with exactly $64$ prime factors found by
J.~Webster (communicated September 11, 2026) and verified here, so $\Smin \le N$; our own
$148$--digit incumbent $N_0$, found by a GPU exchange search, is approximately $1.03\,N$ and is shown to be the
least $64$--factor Carmichael number whose Carmichael function divides $\lambda(N_0)$. For the lower bound we prove, by a finite
exhaustion over a certified prime universe, that no $64$--factor Carmichael number is smaller than
$10^{145}$. Together,
\[
  10^{145} \;\le\; \Smin \;\le\; N \;<\; 10^{148},
\]
so $\Smin$ has $146$, $147$, or $148$ decimal digits. All arithmetic is exact. The lower bound is
a reproducible computation: the admissible universe is bounded by a Korselt cofactor argument, the
resulting bound is recomputed independently, and the exhaustion is carried out by three separate
enumeration implementations, one of which takes its Korselt verdict at every leaf from a frozen
reference oracle. The same cofactor minimum transfers to the next factor count, giving
$10^{148}\le S_{65}\le U_{65}$ for an explicit verified $151$--digit Carmichael number $U_{65}$, so
the smallest $65$--factor Carmichael number has $149$, $150$, or $151$ digits (\S\ref{sec:s65}). For
every $k$ up to $144$ the same relaxed cofactor bound, computed in one shared pass, gives a certified
lower endpoint that improves the elementary primorial baseline by $16$--$29$ digits, paired with a
verified incumbent as upper endpoint (\S\ref{sec:ladder}). The author is responsible for the
mathematics and code; see the acknowledgements for the role of AI assistance.
\end{abstract}

\section{Statement}

A \emph{Carmichael number} is a composite integer $n$ with $b^{n}\equiv b \pmod n$ for all integers
$b$. By Korselt's criterion, a composite integer $n$ is Carmichael if and only if it is squarefree
and $(p-1)\mid(n-1)$ for every prime $p\mid n$. Write $\omega(n)$ for the number of distinct prime
factors. The sequence $S_k=\min\{\,n : n \text{ Carmichael},\ \omega(n)=k\,\}$ is
OEIS~A006931; values are known for $3\le k\le 63$ (R.~G.~E.~Pinch for the smaller $k$; the
$k=36$--$63$ values from the Butler University computation, collected in J.~Webster's repository
cited below), and $S_{64}$ is open as of September~2026. This note proves:

\begin{theorem}[Interval]\label{thm:interval}
$10^{145}\le \Smin \le N$, where
\[
  \begin{aligned}
  N \;=\;{}& 524394558743645786915728373569633336575821258\\
  &922388902572826761352267639397817207104051794\\
  &486290626375080033731873206687812206750535842\\
  &2938650976001 .
\end{aligned}
\]
is a $148$--digit Carmichael number with exactly $64$ prime factors. Consequently $\Smin$ has
$146$, $147$, or $148$ decimal digits, and the remaining exclusion interval for $\Smin$ is
$[10^{145},N)$.
\end{theorem}

The two bounds are proved in \S\ref{sec:upper} and \S\ref{sec:lower}. We use throughout the
following elementary consequence of Korselt's criterion.

\begin{lemma}[Pairwise admissibility]\label{lem:adm}
If $p<q$ are distinct primes dividing a Carmichael number $n$, then $p\nmid(q-1)$. Moreover $n$ is
odd.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Since $q\mid n$ we have $(q-1)\mid(n-1)$, so $n\equiv 1\pmod{d}$ for every divisor $d$ of $q-1$. If
$p\mid(q-1)$ then $n\equiv 1\pmod p$; but $p\mid n$ gives $n\equiv 0\pmod p$, forcing $p\mid 1$, a
contradiction. Oddness: if $n$ were even then $n-1$ would be odd, so for any odd prime factor $p$
the even number $p-1$ could not divide $n-1$; hence a Carmichael number with $k\ge3$ distinct prime
factors has no factor $2$ and is odd.
\end{proof}

\section{Upper bound}\label{sec:upper}

The number $N$ of Theorem~\ref{thm:interval} is the product of the $64$ primes
\[
\begin{aligned}
&19,\,29,\,31,\,37,\,41,\,43,\,47,\,53,\,61,\,67,\,71,\,73,\,79,\,89,\,97,\,101,\,103,\,109,\,113,\,127,\\
&131,\,139,\,151,\,157,\,163,\,167,\,181,\,193,\,197,\,199,\,211,\,239,\,241,\,251,\,257,\,271,\,277,\,281,\,307,\,313,\\
&331,\,337,\,353,\,379,\,397,\,409,\,421,\,433,\,443,\,449,\,463,\,499,\,521,\,547,\,577,\,599,\,601,\,617,\,631,\,641,\\
&691,\,769,\,1249,\,1579.
\end{aligned}
\]
These are $64$ distinct primes; re-derive $N$ by multiplying the list rather than trusting the
printed decimal. Each factor is at most $1579$, so its primality is certified by exact trial
division (a deterministic check at this size). One verifies directly that $(p-1)\mid(N-1)$ for every
$p$; equivalently, writing $\lambda(N)=\operatorname{lcm}_{p\mid N}(p-1)=155016423578016000$, one has
$N\equiv 1\pmod{\lambda(N)}$. Hence $N$ is a squarefree Carmichael number with $\omega(N)=64$, and
$\Smin\le N$. Since $10^{147}\le N<10^{148}$, the number $N$ has $148$ digits. The certificate is
verified by \code{ref/ref\_carmichael.py::verify\_certificate}, an independent, dependency--free
routine (squarefreeness, distinctness, per--factor primality, and Korselt) sharing no code with the
search that produced $N$.

\begin{remark}[Provenance]\label{rem:prov}
$N$ was found by J.~Webster's CPU search and communicated to the author on September~11, 2026; it is
reproduced here after independent factorisation and verification. It is an upper bound, not claimed
minimal. Our own best incumbent, found by the GPU exchange search of \S\ref{sec:exchange} at radius
$r=8$ and modulus $M_0=1768248177696000$, is the $148$--digit number
\[
  N_0 \;\approx\; 1.0295\,N \quad (N_0/N=1.029452793\ldots),\qquad \lambda(N_0)=M_0,\quad \Pp(N_0)=1249,
\]
whose $64$ factors are listed in the repository file\\
\code{results\_k64\_best\_global\_prev\_148\_539839443357.json};
$N$ and $N_0$ share $53$ prime factors. $N_0$ had improved our previous $148$--digit incumbent
(largest prime $3697$, radius $7$, same modulus) by a factor $1.605$ and an earlier $149$--digit
candidate. The $148$ digits of $N$ are consistent with the known neighbours only in the following

candidate has $151$ digits, hence exceeds $N$. This uses the proved interval and the stated digit
lengths; it is not an appeal to any general monotonicity of $S_k$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The exchange search and a restricted minimum}\label{sec:exchange}

Fix a modulus $M$ and let $Q(M)=\{q \text{ prime}: (q-1)\mid M,\ q\nmid M\}$. If $n$ is composite and
squarefree with every prime factor in $Q(M)$ and $n\equiv 1\pmod M$, then $(q-1)\mid M\mid(n-1)$ for
every $q\mid n$, so $n$ is Carmichael by Korselt. The converse holds only for the exact modulus: every
Carmichael number $n$ with $\lambda(n)=D$ has all its prime factors in $Q(D)$ (a prime factor $q$ of a
Carmichael number never divides $\lambda(n)$, by Lemma~\ref{lem:adm}) and satisfies $n\equiv1\pmod D$.
Consequently searching every divisor $D$ of $M$ covers every Carmichael number with $\lambda(n)\mid M$,
whereas a search at $M$ alone does not (for instance $\lambda(561)=80\mid160$ but $561\not\equiv1\pmod{160}$).

\paragraph{Exchange meet--in--the--middle.}
Choose a base $B_0\subset Q(M)$ of $64$ primes with product $P_B$, and a radius $r$. A
\emph{radius-$r$ exchange} replaces $D\subset B_0$ by $I\subset Q(M)\setminus B_0$ with $|D|=|I|=r$,
giving $n=P_B\,P_I/P_D$ where $P_I=\prod I$, $P_D=\prod D$. The condition $n\equiv1\pmod M$ reads
$P_B P_I\equiv P_D\pmod M$, so the search is a join of the multiset $\{P_D \bmod M\}$ over deletions
with $\{P_B P_I\bmod M\}$ over insertions. In the implementation the deletion side is generated on the
CPU (with the product filter below) and held resident on the GPU, the insertion side is streamed from
a CPU generator and sorted and joined on the GPU (very small instances, and the $134$--bit case at
radius $14$, are joined on the host with exact integers); every residue match is checked with exact
integer arithmetic, and every candidate below the bound is verified by the reference oracle. An
\emph{improving} completion is one with $n<U$ for the current bound $U$.

\begin{lemma}[Product bounds]\label{lem:prod}
Let $U$ be a positive integer bound, $r\ge1$, let $D_{\max,r}$ be the product of the $r$ largest primes
of $B_0$ and $I_{\min,r}$ the product of the $r$ smallest primes of $Q(M)\setminus B_0$ (empty products
are $1$). Every improving radius-$r$ completion satisfies
\[
  P_I \;<\; I_{\mathrm{cap}}:=\Big\lfloor\frac{U\,D_{\max,r}}{P_B}\Big\rfloor+1
  \qquad\text{and}\qquad
  P_D \;\ge\; D_{\min}:=\Big\lfloor \frac{P_B\,I_{\min,r}}{U}\Big\rfloor+1 ,
\]
the largest prime of $I$ is at most $\lfloor (I_{\mathrm{cap}}-1)/I_{\min,r-1}\rfloor$, and if the
insertion primes are split at a threshold $y$ into $a_1<a_2<\cdots\le y<c_1<c_2<\cdots$, a completion
using exactly $h$ primes above $y$ satisfies $n\ge (P_B/D_{\max,r})\prod_{j\le r-h}a_j\prod_{j\le h}c_j$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
$n<U$ with $P_D\le D_{\max,r}$ gives $P_I<U D_{\max,r}/P_B$, hence $P_I\le\lfloor U D_{\max,r}/P_B\rfloor$;
with $P_I\ge I_{\min,r}$ it gives $P_D>P_B I_{\min,r}/U$. The largest prime of $I$ times the product of
the other $r-1$ insertion primes, which is at least $I_{\min,r-1}$, is $P_I\le I_{\mathrm{cap}}-1$; the
stratified bound is the stratified minimum of $P_I$. (A completion admitted by the integer rounding but
not improving is discarded by the final test $n<U$.)
\end{proof}

The second bound is what makes large radii affordable: at $r=9$ for $N_0$ it keeps $40\,966\,814$ of
the $\binom{64}{9}=27\,540\,584\,512$ deletion subsets, so the deletion side stays resident on the GPU
while the insertion side is streamed through it.

\begin{lemma}[Per--modulus exactness]\label{lem:exact}
Let $D\mid M$ with $|Q(D)|\ge 64$, let $B_0(D)$ be the $64$ smallest primes of $Q(D)$, $T_{64}$ their
product and $T_{63}=T_{64}/\max B_0(D)$, and suppose $T_{64}<U$. Let $S\subset Q(D)$ be any $64$-subset
with $\prod S<U$. Then every prime of $S$ is at most $\lfloor (U-1)/T_{63}\rfloor$, and
$|S\setminus B_0(D)|\le r_{\max}(D)$, where, with $a_1<a_2<\cdots$ the primes of $Q(D)\setminus B_0(D)$,
$b_1>b_2>\cdots>b_{64}$ the primes of $B_0(D)$ in decreasing order, $R(D):=\min(64,|Q(D)|-64)$,
$L_0:=T_{64}$ and $L_r:=T_{64}\prod_{j\le r}a_j/\prod_{j\le r}b_j$ for $1\le r\le R(D)$,
\[
  r_{\max}(D)\;:=\;\max\{\,r\in\mathbb Z:\ 0\le r\le R(D),\ L_r<U\,\}
\]
(well defined, since $L_0=T_{64}<U$). In particular the exchange search from $B_0(D)$ at radii $0,\dots,r_{\max}(D)$
over the pool $\{q\in Q(D): q\le (U-1)/T_{63}\}$ finds every Carmichael number $n<U$ with
$\omega(n)=64$ and $\lambda(n)=D$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The $63$ smallest elements of $S$ multiply to at least $T_{63}$, which bounds the largest by
$(U-1)/T_{63}$. If $|S\setminus B_0(D)|=r$ then $r\le\min(64,|Q(D)|-64)$ and $\prod S\ge L_r$, because the
$r$ primes outside are at least $a_1,\dots,a_r$ and the $64-r$ primes inside are at least the
$64-r$ smallest of $B_0(D)$; and whenever $r+1$ is in range, $L_{r+1}/L_r=a_{r+1}/b_{r+1}>1$ because every
prime outside $B_0(D)$ exceeds every prime inside. Hence $r>r_{\max}(D)$ forces $\prod S\ge L_r\ge
L_{r_{\max}(D)+1}\ge U$. The last sentence follows from the first paragraph of this subsection.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[A restricted minimum]\label{thm:restricted}
$N_0$ is the least Carmichael number with exactly $64$ prime factors whose Carmichael function divides
$M_0=\lambda(N_0)=2^8\cdot3^5\cdot5^3\cdot7^2\cdot11\cdot13\cdot17\cdot23\cdot83$. Consequently every
$64$--factor Carmichael number below $N_0$---in particular $N$---has a prime factor $q$ with
$(q-1)\nmid M_0$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
If $\lambda(n)\mid M_0$ then $\lambda(n)=D$ for a divisor $D$ of $M_0$. Of the $20\,736$ divisors,
$111$ satisfy the hypothesis $T_{64}<N_0$ of Lemma~\ref{lem:exact} (the others cannot carry such an
$n$ at all); their pool caps and radii $r_{\max}(D)\le 14$ are tabulated in
\code{gpu/mstar\_divisor\_family.json}. For each of the $111$ moduli the exchange search of
Lemma~\ref{lem:exact} was run in full---base $B_0(D)$ checked directly, radii $1,\dots,r_{\max}(D)$
over the complete capped pool with the bounds of Lemma~\ref{lem:prod}, and the residue join
$n\equiv1\pmod D$---for $684$ exchange instances in all, with no incomplete instance and no
completion below $N_0$. The deletion and insertion record counts at $D=M_0$ (for instance
$36\,755\,836$ and $652\,881\,611$ at radius $9$, and $13$ and $23$ at radius $14$) agree exactly with an
independent enumeration.
\end{proof}

Webster's $N$ illustrates the corollary: exactly one of its factors, $1579$ ($1578=2\cdot3\cdot263$),
has $(q-1)\nmid M_0$, and $\lambda(N)=M_0\cdot 263/3$.
The same census and search were then run for $N$ itself. $\lambda(N)=2^8\cdot3^4\cdot5^3\cdot7^2\cdot11\cdot13\cdot17\cdot23\cdot83\cdot263$ has $34\,560$
divisors, of which $118$ satisfy $T_{64}<N$ (radii $r_{\max}(D)\le 13$; table
\code{gpu/divisor\_family\_lambdaJ.json}); all $118$ were searched in full ($728$ exchange instances plus
$118$ base checks, none incomplete) with no completion below $N$. Hence:

\begin{theorem}[A restricted minimum for $N$]\label{thm:restrictedN}
$N$ is the least Carmichael number with exactly $64$ prime factors whose Carmichael function divides
$\lambda(N)$; every $64$--factor Carmichael number below $N$ has a prime factor $q$ with
$(q-1)\nmid\lambda(N)$ and a prime factor $q'$, not necessarily distinct from $q$, with $(q'-1)\nmid M_0$
(the latter by Theorem~\ref{thm:restricted}, since $n<N<N_0$).
\end{theorem}

Both restricted minima are statements about a divisor family, not about $\Smin$. The census script
(\code{gpu/divisor\_family\_census.py}) is standard--library Python and independent of the GPU code;
it discards a divisor with fewer than $64$ eligible primes below its sieve bound only after checking
that no completion with primes beyond the bound could be below $U$.

\paragraph{What the two incumbents show.}
Theorems~\ref{thm:restricted} and~\ref{thm:restrictedN} make precise where a fixed--modulus
exchange search is blind. Within the family $\lambda(n)\mid M$ the search is exhaustive, so no
choice of base, radius or insertion pool at $M$ or at any divisor of $M$ can reach a number
outside it; Webster's $N$ lies outside the family of $N_0$ by exactly one prime power ($263$,
through $1579$), and no search at $M_0$ could have found it. For the two families searched here the
lever for further improvement is therefore the modulus rather than the radius (in other, incompletely
searched families a larger radius can still matter). Once a candidate $\lambda$ is proposed, the
census and the search of Lemma~\ref{lem:exact} either produce a smaller number or certify that none
exists in that family; for the two families here this took minutes per modulus, an observation about
these computations rather than a bound for arbitrary moduli, whose pools and feasible radii may be much
larger. Webster's number supplied a second modulus to investigate. Precisely what the two restricted
minima establish is
\[
  n<N,\ n\text{ a $64$--factor Carmichael number}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  \lambda(n)\nmid M_0\ \text{ and }\ \lambda(n)\nmid\lambda(N).
\]
This leaves considerable uncertainty about both the size and the Carmichael function of $\Smin$: the
certified lower bound of \S\ref{sec:lower} is $10^{145}$ (with $10^{146}$ at the tier of the
strengthening remark in \S4), and $N$ exceeds $10^{146}$ by a factor of about $52$.

\section{Lower bound}\label{sec:lower}

Fix $Y=10^{145}$. We show no $64$--factor Carmichael number lies below $Y$.

\subsection{A certified prime universe}

\begin{lemma}[Cofactor lower bound]\label{lem:C}
There is an explicit constant
\[
  \begin{aligned}
  C \;=\;{}& 181214059509297180446495905961257618839971352\\
  &500899762007368983685095561308686715879985893\\
  &492155734750437055024004408677366582371670140\\
  &3213549 .
\end{aligned}
\]
(a $142$--digit integer) such that for every Carmichael number $n$ with $\omega(n)=64$ and every
prime factor $q\mid n$, the product of the remaining $63$ factors satisfies $n/q\ge C$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[Proof (finite computation)]
Fix such an $n$ and a prime factor $q$; let $p_1<\dots<p_{63}$ be the other factors, all odd
(Lemma~\ref{lem:adm}) and pairwise admissible. Let $S$ be the set of these that are $\le 97$; there
are finitely many internally admissible such $S$. For a fixed $S$, let $b_1(S)<b_2(S)<\cdots$ be the
primes $>97$ that are \emph{individually} admissible with every element of $S$ (i.e.\ $p\nmid(b-1)$
for all $p\in S$). The actual cofactor tail $r_1<\dots<r_{63-|S|}$ of $n$ consists of primes $>97$
each admissible with $S$, so $r_i\ge b_i(S)$ for every $i$. Hence
\[
  n/q \;=\; \prod_{p\in S}p \cdot \prod_{i=1}^{63-|S|} r_i
  \;\ge\; F(S) \;:=\; \Big(\prod_{p\in S}p\Big)\prod_{i=1}^{63-|S|} b_i(S)
  \;\ge\; \min_{S} F(S) \;=\; C.
\]
Three points make this rigorous: (i) the $b_i(S)$ need not be pairwise admissible with one another,
but ignoring those further constraints only decreases the product, so $F(S)$ is a genuine lower
bound; (ii) the computation uses a finite prime table (odd primes $\le 10^4$) and checks, for every
enumerated $S$, that it contains enough eligible tail primes, so no smaller completion is truncated
away; (iii) the cofactor $n/q$ itself need not be Carmichael. The minimum is taken over all
$407760$ admissible $S$. Thus $C$ is a certified lower bound on the cofactor product; it is not
claimed to be the smallest possible admissible $63$--factor product.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Universe]\label{cor:univ}
Every $64$--factor Carmichael number $n<Y$ has largest prime factor
\[
  \Pp(n)\;=\;q\;\le\;\Big\lfloor \tfrac{Y-1}{C}\Big\rfloor \;=\; 5518 .
\]
In particular every prime factor of such an $n$ lies among the $727$ odd primes $\le 5518$ (the
largest being $5507$).
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Take $q=\Pp(n)$ the largest factor; then $n=q\cdot(n/q)\ge q\,C$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:C}, and $n<Y$
gives $q\le(Y-1)/C$; evaluate the floor. Every other factor is smaller, hence also $\le 5518$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Exhaustion}

\begin{theorem}[Lower bound]\label{thm:lower}
There is no Carmichael number $n<10^{145}$ with $\omega(n)=64$. Hence $\Smin\ge 10^{145}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof (finite exhaustion)]
By Corollary~\ref{cor:univ} every candidate is a product of $64$ distinct primes drawn from the
universe $U$ of $727$ odd primes $\le 5518$, with product $<Y$. Enumerate all increasing
$64$--subsets of $U$ with product $<Y$, pruning a partial choice only by two \emph{necessary}
conditions: (i) fewer than the required number of primes remain; and (ii) the current product times
the $64-h$ smallest still-available primes individually compatible with the $h$ already chosen
already reaches $Y$ (an exact--integer $R_{\min}$ test). Pairwise admissibility
(Lemma~\ref{lem:adm}) is enforced during descent---once $p$ is chosen, every later prime $q$ with
$p\mid(q-1)$ is discarded---which removes no genuine Carmichael candidate, since any real completion
survives pairwise filtering and its remaining factors lie in the current available pool. At each
complete $64$--product, test Korselt.

The enumeration terminates with exactly $127092$ increasing pairwise--admissible $64$--subsets of
$U$ whose product is below $Y$ (equivalently $127092$ distinct products, by unique factorization),
of which \emph{none} is Carmichael. Therefore no $64$--factor Carmichael number is smaller than
$10^{145}$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Reproducibility}\label{sec:repro}

The exhaustion was carried out by \emph{three separate enumeration implementations}, and the
universe bound $C$ was \emph{independently recomputed}; all agree. Everything below is at repository
revision \code{f872e32} of \href{https://github.com/AlexanderEastwood/carmichael-frontier}{\code{AlexanderEastwood/carmichael-frontier}}.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \code{s64\_lower\_bound\_certificate.py} runs two enumerators: a binary include/exclude walk
with an incrementally maintained $\operatorname{lcm}(p_i-1)$ Korselt test ($327049$ reference
nodes), and an ordered--choice walk using per--prime divisibility, a separate prime generator, and
no bitmasks ($163525$ nodes). Both report $127092$ admissible products and $0$ Carmichael. They use
different prime generators, traversal logic, and Korselt predicates, while \emph{sharing} the
cofactor--bound computation and the results/hash reporting code; the identical order--sensitive leaf
digest \code{sha256 = e05f62598eea784b3f60f025c6ccbf30700115ecbef42702e24154dcece32321} is therefore
a reproducibility aid, not an additional independent correctness check.
\item \code{carmichael\_prime\_bound.py} independently recomputes $C$ and the cap $\Pp\le 5518$ from
first principles. It does \emph{not} enumerate products or establish the absence of Carmichael
numbers; it certifies only the universe.
\item \code{s64\_lower\_bound\_oracle\_check.py} is a separate clean--room enumerator (its own

\section{The interval}

Combining Theorem~\ref{thm:lower} (lower) with \S\ref{sec:upper} (upper) proves
Theorem~\ref{thm:interval}: $10^{145}\le\Smin\le N<10^{148}$, so $\Smin$ has $146$, $147$, or $148$
digits, with remaining exclusion interval $[10^{145},N)$. This is a global statement about $\Smin$;
it is \emph{not} a determination of $\Smin$, which would require excluding every $64$--factor
Carmichael number in $[10^{145},N)$.

\begin{remark}[Strengthening to $147$--$148$ digits]
A complete exact--$k$ preproduct search over all $64$--factor products $\le 10^{146}$ returns no
Carmichael number, giving $10^{146}<\Smin\le N$, i.e.\ $\Smin$ has $147$ or $148$ digits. This is a
different tool from the enumerators of \S\ref{sec:repro} (a depth--first preproduct search with an
exact $R_{\min}$ bound and the $P\lambda(P)>B$ arithmetic--progression switch), and it is far cheaper
than the $\ge 3.89\times10^{10}$ admissible--product count below $10^{146}$ because it rejects
prefixes without materialising each product ($\approx 71$\,s, single core). \emph{Verification
tier}: the $\Smin\ge 10^{145}$ bound of Theorem~\ref{thm:lower} is the triple--verified core; this
strengthening currently rests on the search engine, corroborated by two independent switch strategies
both returning ``none'' and by the same engine reproducing ``none'' at $10^{145}$ (a fourth
independent confirmation of that bound) and reproducing $S_{10}\dots S_{29}$ against
OEIS~A006931. An independent admissibility certificate at $10^{146}$ is the outstanding follow--up;
the full exclusion of $[10^{145},N)$ that would prove $\Smin=N$ remains a machine--scale endgame.
\end{remark}

\section{Transfer to $S_{65}$}\label{sec:s65}

The cofactor machinery transfers to $k=65$ with no new exhaustion. Let
\[
  A_{64}\;=\;\min\{\,\textstyle\prod_{i=1}^{64}p_i : p_1<\dots<p_{64}\text{ odd primes, pairwise admissible}\,\}
\]
be the least product of $64$ pairwise--admissible odd primes. The enumeration of \S\ref{sec:lower}
records it as a by--product: the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:C} never uses that the cofactor belongs to a
Carmichael number, so it bounds every pairwise--admissible $63$--product by $C$; hence every admissible
$64$--product below $10^{145}$ has all its primes $\le5518$ and is among the $127092$ enumerated
products, and the minimum over the enumeration is the global $A_{64}$ (that this minimum lies below
$10^{145}$ is witnessed by those products). It is the explicit $145$--digit integer
\[
  \begin{aligned}
  A_{64} \;=\;{}& 441172365445881169056428109132100061485293342\\
  &780359484110673067879903768586477795940804550\\
  &206689631876408186963205931713339027583328239\\
  &6201943377 .
\end{aligned}
\]

\begin{lemma}[Cofactor transfer]\label{lem:transfer}
For every Carmichael number $n$ with $\omega(n)=65$ and every prime $q\mid n$, the remaining $64$
factors form a pairwise--admissible $64$--product (Lemma~\ref{lem:adm}), so $n/q\ge A_{64}$. Hence any
such $n<U$ has $\Pp(n)\le\lfloor(U-1)/A_{64}\rfloor$.
\end{lemma}

Taking $U=10^{148}$ gives $\Pp\le 2266$: a universe of $334$ odd primes.

\begin{theorem}[Lower bound for $S_{65}$]\label{thm:s65}
There is no Carmichael number $n<10^{148}$ with $\omega(n)=65$. Hence $S_{65}\ge 10^{148}$, and the
smallest $65$--factor Carmichael number has at least $149$ digits.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof (finite exhaustion)]
By Lemma~\ref{lem:transfer} every candidate is a product of $65$ distinct primes from the $334$--prime
universe with product $<10^{148}$. An ordered--prefix search with exact product pruning descends until
$P\lambda(P)>10^{148}$ and then tests the at most one cofactor in the forced residue class
$R\equiv P^{-1}\pmod{\lambda(P)}$, enforcing $65$ distinct factors and full Korselt with primality
taken from the sieve. It visits $64355$ nodes, forms $66$ progression candidates, and finds no
Carmichael number.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Reproduction and verification tier]
Theorem~\ref{thm:s65} was reproduced four ways, all agreeing on $0$ Carmichael numbers:
(i) the certificate \code{s65/s65\_exclusion.py} above; (ii) a separate native binary
enumeration \code{s65/verify65.cpp} that checks all $22{,}287{,}138$ admissible $65$--products below
$10^{148}$ without inverses or the progression shortcut; and (iii)--(iv) our preproduct search engine
in both switch modes (\S\ref{sec:repro}), which independently reports $n=$ none with matching
counts ($5012$ inversions, $66$ progression candidates). A companion certificate
\code{s65/s65\_transfer\_certificate.py} establishes the weaker $S_{65}\ge 3.17\times10^{147}$ by
extending the recorded $64$--products two independent ways ($285$ admissible $65$--products, $0$
Carmichael). All rest on the same $A_{64}$, whose defining $127092$--product set and digest are the
triple--verified core of \S\ref{sec:lower}.
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}[Interval for $S_{65}$]\label{thm:s65interval}
$10^{148}\le S_{65}\le U_{65}$, where $U_{65}$ is the $151$--digit Carmichael number with exactly $65$
prime factors $19,31,37,\dots,1021,1993$ (Webster's candidate). Consequently $S_{65}$ has $149$,
$150$, or $151$ decimal digits.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The lower bound is Theorem~\ref{thm:s65}. For the upper bound: by Lemma~\ref{lem:transfer} every
$65$--factor Carmichael below $10^{151}$ has all prime factors $\le\lfloor(10^{151}-1)/A_{64}\rfloor=
2266687$, so $U_{65}$ (being $<10^{151}$) factors completely over that range. Independent trial
division confirms $U_{65}$ is a product of exactly $65$ distinct primes $\le 1993$; the frozen oracle
\code{ref/ref\_carmichael.py::verify\_certificate} confirms Korselt, so $U_{65}$ is Carmichael with
$\omega=65$ and $S_{65}\le U_{65}$. As $U_{65}$ has $151$ digits and $S_{65}\ge10^{148}$ has at least
$149$, the interval follows. (Factor list in \code{results\_k65\_webster\_verified.json}.)
\end{proof}

\section{An interval ladder for $k=64,\dots,144$}\label{sec:ladder}

Lemma~\ref{lem:C} is an instance of a bound available at every factor count. For $m\ge1$ and a
cutoff $y$, let $\mathcal H_y$ be the internally admissible subsets of the odd primes $\le y$, and for
$S\in\mathcal H_y$ let $b_1(S)<b_2(S)<\cdots$ be the primes $>y$ individually admissible with every
element of $S$. Define
\[
  C_m(y)\;=\;\min_{\substack{S\in\mathcal H_y\\ |S|\le m}}
  \Big(\prod_{p\in S}p\Big)\prod_{i=1}^{m-|S|} b_i(S),
\]
and
\[
  A_m\;=\;\min\Big\{\prod_{i=1}^{m}p_i : p_1<\dots<p_m\text{ odd, pairwise admissible}\Big\}.
\]

\begin{theorem}[Ladder]\label{thm:ladder}
For every $m\ge3$ for which $S_m$ exists, $C_m(y)\le A_m\le S_m$. Hence, for each $k$ with a verified incumbent $U_k$,
$C_k(y)\le S_k\le U_k$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
$A_m\le S_m$ because every Carmichael number is squarefree with pairwise admissible prime factors
(Lemma~\ref{lem:adm}), so a $k$--factor Carmichael number is in particular an admissible
$k$--product. $C_m(y)\le A_m$ is the argument of Lemma~\ref{lem:C} with $63$ replaced by $m$: for an
admissible $m$--product write $S$ for its primes $\le y$; its tail primes exceed $y$ and are each
admissible with $S$, so the $i$--th smallest is $\ge b_i(S)$, and ignoring the tail's mutual
constraints only lowers the product. Coverage requires enough eligible tail primes for every class,
which is checked.
\end{proof}

The three quantities are deliberately kept distinct: $C_m(y)$ is a certified relaxed bound (what
the table uses), $A_m$ the exact admissible minimum (a separate optimisation, not needed here and not
certifiable by the Carmichael residue shortcut, since an admissible product need not satisfy
$n\equiv1\pmod{\lambda(n)}$), and $S_m$ the Carmichael minimum. A completed Carmichael exclusion
below $Y$ strengthens a row's lower endpoint; it is \emph{not} a bound on admissible products and must
not be substituted into a cofactor formula.

All $C_m(97)$ for $63\le m\le144$ were obtained in one shared pass over the $407760$ classes of
$\mathcal H_{97}$ (tail primes $\le10^4$, the largest actually needed being $3209$; exact integer
arithmetic, $\approx10$\,s). $C_{63}(97)$ reproduces the constant of Lemma~\ref{lem:C}, and
$C_{64},C_{100},C_{137},C_{144}$ agree with an independent computation. The upper bounds $U_k$ are
verified $k$--factor Carmichael numbers: $U_{64}=N$ and $U_{65}$ are Webster's (\S\ref{sec:upper},
\S\ref{sec:s65}), the rows $k=66,\dots,144$ come from our search; each is re--checked by the frozen
oracle. Table~\ref{tab:ladder} lists the resulting
digit intervals; the rows $k=64,65$ carry the stronger completed--exclusion endpoints of
\S\ref{sec:lower}--\S\ref{sec:s65}. The elementary primorial baseline is shown for comparison; the
relaxed bound improves it by $16$--$29$ digits across the ladder (column ``gain''). This is the
improvement we can identify and quantify: the product of the first $k$ odd primes is the elementary
lower bound available without the admissibility argument, and for $k\ge 64$ we know of no published
lower bound on $S_k$ to compare against. The \emph{exact} endpoints---the full integers $C_k(97)$,
the incumbents $U_k$ with factor lists, sources, and verification flags---are the certificate and are
recorded per row in \code{ladder/ladder\_manifest.json}, generated by \code{ladder/gen\_ladder.py};
